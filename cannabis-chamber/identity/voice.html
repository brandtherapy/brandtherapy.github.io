Voice

How the brand speaks. Principles first, then what's banned, then exemplars.

01
Operating rules

Voice principles

Voice is part of brand identity. The strategic decision about Personality (P5) lives in the brand strategy; the operational voice rules live here. See the voice exemplar bank for the full paired set of examples.

Principles

  • Rational over emotional
  • Institutional over casual
  • Clear over clever
  • Transparent over polished
  • Authoritative over playful
  • Welcoming but selective
  • Serious without being cold
  • Lead with the standard, then the reason
  • Plain business language over marketing language
  • Concise declarative sentences
  • Contrast moves: standards over novelty, movement over reach, leverage over visibility, deals over drinks, insight over impressions
  • Operator-led practicality: speak like people responsible for outcomes, not consultants

Banned characters

  • em-dash (U+2014)
  • en-dash (U+2013)

(Plain hyphen-minus - is fine in compound words, list bullets, signatures, ranges, and titles.)

Banned phrases

  • "Cannabis disruption" / "We're disrupting cannabis"
  • "Vibes" as a measure of value
  • "Family" as vague community language
  • "Welcome to the community" without specifying who fits
  • "Stoner" / "weed" / "420" references in serious copy
  • "Get seen" / "Stand out" / visibility-first framing
  • Generic networking phrases ("connect with industry insiders," "tap into our network")
  • "Disrupt" / "unlock" / "leverage" as marketing buzzwords

Emojis

Default: no emojis in Chamber content. Approved exceptions:

  • Standard business or check-mark emojis (e.g., ✅) in social-media copy only
  • No decorative emoji stacks. One per post maximum.

Signatures (CCC-specific)

  • Long-form openers: state the standard or the contrast in the first sentence
  • Long-form closers: name the action the reader takes (apply, attend, contact)
  • The Chamber's institutional voice does not use first-person founder signatures

Voice exemplars (brief)

For the full exemplar bank, see the voice exemplar bank.

On-voice signals:

  • "Serious operators do not need more noise. They need the right room, the right context, and the right standard.": direct, declarative, operator-addressed
  • "The Chamber exists to move cannabis business forward, not to fill another calendar.": contrast move, business outcomes framed against activity
  • "Standards first. Novelty second. Everything else is decoration.": triplet rhythm, blunt closure, sets the operating principle
  • "Deals over drinks. Insight over impressions. Movement over reach. That is the business standard the Chamber is here to raise.": contrast triplet + restated standard

Off-voice traps:

  • "Join the hottest cannabis networking community and vibe with industry insiders.": consumer hype, generic networking, lifestyle cue
  • "We're here to disrupt cannabis with next-level events and amazing connections.": disruption claim without proof, vague "amazing," marketing fluff
  • "Get seen by everyone in the cannabis space.": visibility-first, "everyone" overreach, anti-thesis of the Chamber

Voice Exemplars: The Cannabis Chamber of Commerce

The paired bank of on-voice and off-voice samples. This file is the calibration test for any new CCC copy. Compare new drafts against the lists here before publishing.

1. Voice Center

Standards. The non-negotiable HOW. Every sentence either elevates the standard, creates an opportunity, or corrects a misconception. If it does none of the three, cut it.

The voice is the visible artifact of CCC's operating principle: standards-first, novelty second. Rational, transparent, operator-led, authoritative, direct, serious, useful, welcoming to people doing real work.

2. Voice Traits

Trait Meaning
Authority Sets standards clearly and without apology.
Clarity Explains the business logic behind every position.
Transparency Says what qualifies, what does not, and why.
Operator-led practicality Speaks like people responsible for outcomes, not consultants selling theory.
Serious welcome Accessible to those committed to the work, selective against low-commitment attention seekers.

3. Vocabulary

Preferred language (use freely; these signal the voice):

Preferred Why it works
Standards The operating principle, named directly.
Authority The role the Chamber claims and earns.
Trust The currency between members and the public.
Operators The people the Chamber serves; never "members" alone, never "users."
Decision-makers The audience qualifier for serious convenings.
Business community Distinguishes from consumer cannabis community.
Opportunity The output members get from membership.
Convening The Chamber's word for events — heavier than "meetup."
Leadership Earned, not claimed.
Advocacy Strategic; tied to policy outcomes.
Clarity A virtue in itself.
Movement The measure of real impact, not "reach."
Peer benchmarks The Chamber's data product.
Serious work The qualifier for who fits.

Banned language (avoid these; they break the voice):

  • Stoner culture cues; weed puns; 420 references
  • Hype language ("amazing," "next-level," "insane," "game-changing")
  • "Disruption" claims without proof
  • "Family" as vague community language
  • Vibes-first lifestyle copy
  • Cheap exclusivity ("only the best," "VIP-only" without earned meaning)
  • Generic networking clichés ("connect with insiders," "tap into our network")
  • Consultant-speak ("unlock," "synergize," "leverage" as buzzword)
  • Consumer cannabis lifestyle cues (smoke clouds, party scenes, leaf graphics in copy)

4. On-voice examples by channel

LinkedIn / public posts

  • "Serious operators do not need more noise. They need the right room, the right context, and the right standard."
  • "The Chamber exists to move cannabis business forward, not to fill another calendar."
  • "Standards first. Novelty second. Everything else is decoration."
  • "Cannabis does not need more visibility without standards. It needs serious operators in serious rooms, building trust that lasts beyond the next event."
  • "Deals over drinks. Insight over impressions. Movement over reach. That is the business standard the Chamber is here to raise."

Email

Subject line example: "A room built for serious cannabis business"

Opening paragraph example: "The next opportunity rarely comes from more noise. It comes from the right room, the right context, and the right standard. That is what the Chamber is built to create."

Website / membership copy

  • "The Cannabis Chamber of Commerce connects serious cannabis and hemp leaders through executive networking, strategic advocacy, and industry education. Built for operators who move markets, not people browsing directories."
  • "Establish your presence with a serious business community built around standards, access, and opportunity."
  • "Be in the rooms where cannabis business relationships actually move."

Advocacy briefs

The Chamber's advocacy voice frames policy as business standard. Lead with what standard is at stake. Avoid activist framing or partisan language. Cite operators, not consumers.

Sales / member onboarding

  • "Membership is leverage. Not visibility. Operators who join the Chamber qualify on what they bring, not what they want to be seen doing."
  • "If you treat the Chamber as a lead list, this is not the room for you. If you're trying to build the next decade of cannabis business, the door is open."

5. Off-voice library

These are anti-examples. Every line breaks the voice in a specific way. Use them to calibrate what NOT to write.

Generic hype / disruption

  • ❌ "We're here to disrupt cannabis with next-level events and amazing connections."

Breaks: "disruption" without proof; "amazing" as vibes word; generic networking.

Consumer cannabis / lifestyle

  • ❌ "Join the hottest cannabis networking community and vibe with industry insiders."

Breaks: "vibe," "hottest," consumer framing, "insiders" as gloss.

  • ❌ "Light up your cannabis business with our exclusive community."

Breaks: weed pun, cheap exclusivity claim.

Visibility-first framing

  • ❌ "Get seen by everyone in the cannabis space."

Breaks: visibility-first, "everyone" overreach, anti-thesis of the Chamber.

  • ❌ "Stand out from the cannabis crowd and grow your brand."

Breaks: "stand out" frames brand-as-performance; "the cannabis crowd" reduces audience.

Generic community / networking

  • ❌ "Build your network in cannabis."

Breaks: vague, anyone-could-say-this.

  • ❌ "Connect with industry insiders and unlock your potential."

Breaks: "unlock potential" is coaching speak; "insiders" is gloss.

6. Structural patterns

The contrast move

Pair what matters with what doesn't:

  • "Standards over novelty"
  • "Movement over reach"
  • "Leverage over visibility"
  • "Deals over drinks"
  • "Insight over impressions"
  • "Operators over directory browsers"

Use sparingly — one contrast pair per piece keeps it sharp; three or more reads as formula.

The triplet

Three short declaratives, increasing weight, closing with the standard:

  • "Standards first. Novelty second. Everything else is decoration."
  • "Deals over drinks. Insight over impressions. Movement over reach."

The qualifier

Name who the Chamber is for AND who it is not for in the same paragraph:

  • "Built for operators who move markets, not people browsing directories."
  • "If you treat the Chamber as a lead list, this is not for you."

The reason-first lead

Lead with what standard is at stake, then explain. Never lead with a feature or event.
