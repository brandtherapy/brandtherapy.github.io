Voice

How the brand speaks. Principles first, then what's banned, then exemplars.

01
Operating rules

Voice principles

Voice is part of brand identity. The strategic decision about Personality (P5) lives in STRATEGY.md; the operational voice rules live here. See references/voice-exemplars.md for the full paired exemplar bank.

Principles

  • Rational over emotional
  • Institutional over casual
  • Clear over clever
  • Transparent over polished
  • Authoritative over playful
  • Welcoming but selective
  • Serious without being cold
  • Explain the relevant standard and its business purpose
  • Plain business language over marketing language
  • Concise declarative sentences
  • Explain the business purpose with concrete facts and actions. A heading should name its subject or the decision the reader needs to make.
  • Use connected sentences. Avoid slogan pairs, fragment triplets and manufactured contrasts, including in short headings.
  • Operator-led practicality: speak with responsibility for outcomes and explain practical consequences

Banned characters

  • em-dash (U+2014)
  • en-dash (U+2013)

(Plain hyphen-minus - is fine in compound words, list bullets, signatures, ranges, and titles.)

Banned phrases

  • "Cannabis disruption" / "We're disrupting cannabis"
  • "Vibes" as a measure of value
  • "Family" as vague community language
  • "Welcome to the community" without specifying who fits
  • "Stoner" / "weed" / "420" references in serious copy
  • "Get seen" / "Stand out" / visibility-first framing
  • Generic networking phrases ("connect with industry insiders," "tap into our network")
  • "Disrupt" / "unlock" / "leverage" as marketing buzzwords

Emojis

Default: no emojis in Chamber content. Approved exceptions:

  • Standard business or check-mark emojis (e.g., ✅) in social-media copy only
  • No decorative emoji stacks. One per post maximum.

Signatures (CCC-specific)

  • Long-form openers: state the subject and why it matters to the reader
  • Long-form closers: name the action the reader takes (apply, attend, contact)
  • The Chamber's institutional voice does not use first-person founder signatures

Voice exemplars (brief)

For the full exemplar bank, see references/voice-exemplars.md.

On-voice signals:

  • "Meet cannabis business owners and service providers at the next Chamber event.": names the people and the action
  • "Review the event details and register through the Chamber website.": gives a specific next step
  • "What we need to get started": a heading that identifies the purpose of the section

These examples illustrate wording. Verify the event, audience and registration route before using them in a campaign. The shared anti-pattern rules apply to these examples and to every new draft.

Off-voice traps:

  • "Join the hottest cannabis networking community and vibe with industry insiders.": consumer hype, generic networking, lifestyle cue
  • "We're here to disrupt cannabis with next-level events and amazing connections.": disruption claim without proof, vague "amazing," marketing fluff
  • "Get seen by everyone in the cannabis space.": visibility-first, "everyone" overreach, anti-thesis of the Chamber

Voice Exemplars: The Cannabis Chamber of Commerce

The paired bank of on-voice and off-voice samples. This file is the calibration test for any new CCC copy. Compare new drafts against the lists here before publishing.

1. Voice Center

Explain who the Chamber serves, what it offers and how to participate. Every sentence should help the reader understand the subject or take a relevant action.

The voice is rational, transparent and direct. Explain the business reason for a position and welcome people who can contribute to the work.

2. Voice Traits

Trait Meaning
Authority Sets standards clearly and without apology.
Clarity Explains the business logic behind every position.
Transparency Says what qualifies, what does not, and why.
Operator-led practicality Speaks with responsibility for the outcome and explains practical consequences.
Serious welcome Accessible to those committed to the work, selective against low-commitment attention seekers.

3. Vocabulary

Preferred language (use freely; these signal the voice):

Preferred Why it works
Standards The operating principle, named directly.
Authority The role the Chamber claims and earns.
Trust The currency between members and the public.
Operators The people the Chamber serves; never "members" alone, never "users."
Decision-makers The audience qualifier for serious convenings.
Business community Distinguishes from consumer cannabis community.
Opportunity The output members get from membership.
Convening The Chamber's word for events; use the term only when it fits the context.
Leadership Demonstrated through conduct and responsibility.
Advocacy Strategic; tied to policy outcomes.
Clarity A virtue in itself.
Movement The measure of real impact, not "reach."
Peer benchmarks The Chamber's data product.
Serious work The qualifier for who fits.

Banned language (avoid these; they break the voice):

  • Stoner culture cues; weed puns; 420 references
  • Hype language ("amazing," "next-level," "insane," "game-changing")
  • "Disruption" claims without proof
  • "Family" as vague community language
  • Vibes-first lifestyle copy
  • Cheap exclusivity ("only the best," "VIP-only" without earned meaning)
  • Generic networking clichés ("connect with insiders," "tap into our network")
  • Consultant-speak ("unlock," "synergize," "leverage" as buzzword)
  • Consumer cannabis lifestyle cues (smoke clouds, party scenes, leaf graphics in copy)

4. On-voice examples by channel

LinkedIn / public posts

  • "Meet cannabis business owners and service providers at the next Chamber event."
  • "See the event agenda and registration details on the Chamber website."

Email

Subject line example: "Registration details for the next Chamber event"

Opening paragraph example: "The event page has the agenda and registration details. Please check the date and location before registering."

Website / membership copy

  • "The Chamber brings cannabis professionals together through business networking, education and advocacy."
  • "Review the membership options to see which benefits apply to your business."

Advocacy briefs

Explain the policy question and its consequences for cannabis businesses. Support claims with evidence and distinguish the Chamber's position from reported facts.

Sales / member onboarding

  • "Tell us about your business and the region where you work."
  • "Check the membership requirements before choosing a tier."

These are wording examples. Confirm the relevant event, benefits, eligibility and page destinations before using them in live content.

5. Off-voice library

These are anti-examples. Every line breaks the voice in a specific way. Use them to calibrate what NOT to write.

Generic hype / disruption

  • ❌ "We're here to disrupt cannabis with next-level events and amazing connections."

Breaks: "disruption" without proof; "amazing" as vibes word; generic networking.

Consumer cannabis / lifestyle

  • ❌ "Join the hottest cannabis networking community and vibe with industry insiders."

Breaks: "vibe," "hottest," consumer framing, "insiders" as gloss.

  • ❌ "Light up your cannabis business with our exclusive community."

Breaks: weed pun, cheap exclusivity claim.

Visibility-first framing

  • ❌ "Get seen by everyone in the cannabis space."

Breaks: visibility-first, "everyone" overreach, anti-thesis of the Chamber.

  • ❌ "Stand out from the cannabis crowd and grow your brand."

Breaks: "stand out" frames brand-as-performance; "the cannabis crowd" reduces audience.

Generic community / networking

  • ❌ "Build your network in cannabis."

Breaks: vague, anyone-could-say-this.

  • ❌ "Connect with industry insiders and unlock your potential."

Breaks: "unlock potential" is coaching speak; "insiders" is gloss.

6. Structure

Headings

Name the subject or the next decision in familiar language. Use a short descriptive label when the surrounding diagram supplies the detail.

  • "The shared Chamber workspace"
  • "How an event campaign gets made"
  • "What we need to get started"

Explanations

State the proposal, then explain its purpose or the relevant condition in connected sentences. Preserve useful distinctions when shortening the copy.

Actions

Say what the reader needs to do and where they can do it. Include dates, eligibility or limitations when they affect that action.

Do not use slogan pairs, fragment triplets or contrast formulas to make a sentence sound authoritative. The shared anti-pattern rules apply to headings and body copy.

7. AI safety note

Some on-voice phrases ("standards," "operator-led," "movement," "trust") are vocabulary CCC owns by repetition. Generic LLM output that uses these terms loosely may sound on-voice on first read but reads as cheap when stacked next to authored copy. When AI-generating CCC content:

  1. Pair the keyword with concrete evidence (named operators, specific outcomes, peer benchmarks)
  2. Avoid stacking multiple branded keywords in one sentence ("standards-driven operator-led trusted community" reads as buzzword soup)
  3. Use the descriptive headings and connected explanations above. Read the draft for meaning before judging how concise it sounds.

If a draft feels generic, run it against the off-voice library. If it could be flipped to off-voice with two-word edits, it's not on-voice enough yet.
