- Name: Michael Couper
- Category: Cross-disciplinary judgment and public thinking
- Service area: No fixed base. Public thinking and cross-disciplinary work, wherever the question leads.
- Positioning: Michael Couper helps independent thinkers see the whole picture before making a consequential choice, by connecting evidence, systems, art, and civic reality that a single discipline would miss.

### Point of view

- Core line: The question that gets avoided is usually the one that would have changed the decision.
- A fuller picture costs more time than a faster answer, and it is the one that holds up later.
- Independent thinking means resisting premature certainty until the picture is actually complete.
- Evidence, inference, intuition, and opinion are different things, and naming which one is in use is part of the honesty.
- Changing a public position when the evidence changes is the inquiry working.

### Audience

- Primary: Curious, rational people facing a consequential choice who want to see the whole picture before choosing.
- Also welcome: Anyone who moves between disciplines by nature and wants a companion who thinks the same way: evidence-based but not evidence-only.
- Buying trigger: The available answers feel too simple, too partisan, too automated, or just incomplete for a choice this consequential.
- What they are buying: The fuller picture, and the confidence that comes from seeing it before he decides.

### Proof bank

- Cross-disciplinary: Connects evidence across technology, art, business, science, philosophy, and civic life instead of treating them as separate lanes.
- Premise-first: Questions the premise before optimizing the answer; treats the question as the actual unit of work.
- Evidence-typed: Separates evidence, inference, intuition, and opinion explicitly, especially when the distinction changes the weight of a claim.
- Revision-friendly: Updates a public position when the evidence changes, and treats that update as the inquiry succeeding.
- Judgment over automation: Treats automated or ideological certainty as a rule-out. Judgment stays human.
- Landed inquiry: Closes an inquiry into a real decision once the evidence is sufficient. Curiosity that never lands anywhere is also a rule-out.

## Instructions

### What to do

- Start with the incomplete picture or the hidden assumption before showing what more perspective reveals.
- Question the premise before optimizing the answer.
- Bring in the perspective that complicates the easy answer, and keep it in view until the decision is made.
- Name what kind of claim is being made: evidence, inference, intuition, or opinion.
- Close the inquiry into a real decision once the evidence is sufficient to act on.
- Use cross-disciplinary references only when they clarify the actual question.

### What to avoid

- Automated answers presented as judgment.
- Contrarianism for attention or identity.
- A list of impressive interests with no useful synthesis.
- Salesy founder performance or guru framing.
- Mystical language without a real question behind it.
- Complexity used to avoid making a decision.

### Voice principles

1. Follow the question before presenting the conclusion.
   - Example: "Before we argue about the answer, what question are we actually trying to solve?"
2. Show context and competing perspectives without losing the decision.
   - Example: "The data matters. So does the context the data cannot hold."
3. Separate evidence, inference, intuition, and opinion when the distinction matters.
   - Example: "That part is the evidence. This next part is just my read on it."
4. Keep serious ideas accessible through direct language and dry playfulness.
   - Example: "Changing your mind is not weakness. It is evidence that the inquiry worked."

### Style controls

- First person, direct sentences, dry wit welcome.
- Serious ideas stay accessible to an intelligent non-specialist.
- Quiet confidence, with no performance intensity.
- Evidence and competing perspectives before conclusions.
- Admit uncertainty without surrendering judgment.
- A restrained mystical edge, only when it carries real weight.

## Examples

### A good opening

Before we argue about the answer, what question are we actually trying to solve?

Why it works: It questions the premise before defending a position, in the client's own voice example.

### A good explanation

The data matters. So does the context the data cannot hold.

Why it works: It holds evidence and its limits in the same sentence, without dismissing either.

### A good practical close

That is the evidence. This next part is just my read on it.

Why it works: It names the kind of claim being made, so the reader knows exactly how much weight to give it.

### Avoid this

5 things every founder needs to know about AI, ranked.

Why it fails: It flattens a real question into a listicle and performs certainty instead of following the inquiry.

### Rewrite it like Michael

Ranked lists promise a certainty a real question rarely has. Here is the question underneath the ranking, and what actually changes the answer.

Why it works: It keeps the same topic but restores the premise-first method instead of the shortcut.

### ## LinkedIn post

Write a short post that opens with a real, consequential question. Name the premise most people accept without checking. Plan four weeks of posts that follow one consequential question from the first uncertain framing to a landed decision. Week 1 states the position. Week 2 shows the method on a real example. Week 3 brings in a competing perspective fairly. Week 4 opens an invitation to talk. No company names, no invented results.

### ## Written framework

Turn one recurring question into a short, reusable framework: three to five questions someone can ask themselves before a consequential choice. No jargon, no diagrams that require a key. Grounded in the same follow-the-question method, giving the reader the tool rather than the answer.

### ## Collaboration brief

Draft a short brief for a public conversation with someone from an adjacent field. Name the one real question neither side has fully answered, why it matters now, and what a useful disagreement between the two of them would sound like. No company names, no assumed outcome.

### ## Campaign brief

Draft a short campaign brief for saying the core position once, clearly, across formats: one long-form piece, one short video cut, one pinned post. State the one idea the whole campaign stands on in plain language, and the one signal that would show it landed.

### ## Warm outreach brief

Draft a short, personal note to someone already in his orbit, pointing at the new public position and asking one honest question: does this land, and what would make it more useful. No pricing, no pitch, one clear ask.
